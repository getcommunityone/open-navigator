CommunityOne · Open Navigator

Local decisions shape daily life.

Most people never see them.

Open Navigator reads public meeting records, budgets and nonprofit filings, and shows what local governments decided, with the passage from the record that says so.

Open source · Apache 2.0

John Bowyer · Founder, CommunityOne, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit · communityone.com

Local governments decide property taxes, school start times, policing budgets and zoning. Those decisions are public, but they are buried in agendas, minutes and meeting videos that few people have time to read. Open Navigator is the open-source engine behind CommunityOne. It reads those public records and shows what was decided, and every result carries the passage from the record that backs it up. CommunityOne is a nonprofit I founded; the code is Apache 2.0, so anyone can check it or build on it.

Four audiences, and they ask different questions of the same public record. Residents want to know what was decided near them and what is coming up. Advocates and nonprofit leaders want to follow an issue across places and see the arguments. Journalists and researchers want the record itself, cited and downloadable, at scale. The problem

91,438 local governments. No common record.

Scattered

Each county, city, township, school district and special district publishes agendas, minutes, video and budgets its own way: PDFs, vendor portals, video channels.

Slow to read

One meeting’s minutes can run dozens of pages. Following one issue across a state by hand is not realistic for a resident or a small nonprofit.

Easy to miss

By the time most people hear about a decision, the vote is over and the window to speak has closed.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Government Organization Summary Report: 2022 & 2025 (91,438 local governments in 2025) · 3

The U.S. Census Bureau counted 91,438 local governments in 2025: counties, cities, townships, school districts and special districts. Each one publishes its records its own way. Some post PDFs, some use vendor portals, some only post video. There is no common format and no single place to look. Reading one city's minutes takes real time; following an issue across a whole state by hand isn't realistic for a resident or a small nonprofit. So most people find out about a decision after the vote, when the chance to speak has passed.

What it does

Find it, read it, follow it

Find

One search across meetings, documents, decisions, bills, officials and nonprofits, filtered by place and topic. The label on the page always names the place the results are filtered to.

Read

Every filtered result shows the passage from the record that matched, with the search term highlighted. A quote, never a paraphrase standing in for evidence.

Follow

Story lenses surface what is worth a look, and a personal Close to Home feed follows the places and issues you choose.

Try it: communityone.com · search a topic, pick your state or city, and open any result to see its source.

Three verbs. Find: one search box across meetings, documents, decisions, bills, officials and nonprofits, narrowed by place and topic, and the label on the page always tells you exactly what the results are filtered to, so you never read national results thinking they are local. Read: when a result shows up because of your search term, the tile shows the actual passage from the record with the term highlighted, so you can see why it matched without clicking in. Follow: story lenses, on the next slide, and a Close to Home feed for the places and issues you pick.

Five ways to spot what matters in the record

LensWhat it shows

🤨 Raised EyebrowsUnusual patterns, such as sole-source contracts, reversals and late additions. Flagged for a person to review, not a finding

Lens names and descriptions as shown in the app (web_app/src/pages/HomeV9.tsx) · 5

Most of the public record is routine. The lenses help people find the parts worth reading. Contested shows split votes and heated debate. Money Moves pulls out contracts, grants and budget changes. Raised Eyebrows flags unusual patterns, like a sole-source contract or a late addition to the agenda; it is a prompt for a person to look, never an accusation, and the app says so. Moving Fast shows items moving quicker than usual. Watch Next shows votes coming up, which is where residents can still act.

The policy model

From one vote to the question behind it

Decision

One vote, in one place, on one date, with the speakers and the quotes.

→

Policy question

The question that vote answers, which comes up in many places over time.

→

Arguments

The arguments made for and against it, gathered into one library and linked back to where each was said.

Illustrative example, not a record: a school board vote on later start times is one instance of the question “Should schools start later?”, which links to every place it has come up and the arguments made there.

Approach draws on Key Point Analysis and the Policy Frames Codebook · 6

This is what lets an advocate follow an issue rather than a single meeting. Every decision is one vote in one place. Behind it is a policy question that comes up again and again in different places. And behind the question is a library of the arguments people make for and against it, each linked back to where it was said. The example on the slide is illustrative, not a real record. The approach borrows from research methods: Key Point Analysis, for grouping many arguments into a few key points, and the Policy Frames Codebook, for how issues get framed.

How it works

How a meeting becomes a searchable decision

Discover

Government websites, video channels, meeting portals, Open States

→

Collect

Agendas, minutes, transcripts, budgets, IRS nonprofit filings, Census data

→

Extract

AI reads transcripts into decisions, votes, speakers and quotes

→

Model

dbt layers: bronze, staging, intermediate, marts; records matched across sources

→

Serve

PostgreSQL, a FastAPI API, the React app, open datasets, an MCP server

Architecture: web_docs/docs/architecture.md · data sources: CITATIONS.md · 7

Five steps. Discover finds where each government posts its records. Collect pulls in agendas, minutes, transcripts and budgets, plus IRS nonprofit filings and Census data. Extract uses AI to turn long transcripts into structured decisions, votes, speakers and quotes. Model is where the data gets cleaned and joined, in dbt, in layers from raw to ready, including matching the same city or nonprofit across different sources. Serve puts it in a Postgres database behind an API, the web app, open datasets anyone can download, and an MCP server so AI assistants can query it directly.

Trust

Rules that keep it honest

No made-up numbers

If data is missing, the app says it is missing. No placeholder figures, ever, and above all no invented dollar amounts or vote counts.

Evidence on every match

A filtered result shows the real passage that matched. No passage, no result.

The label matches the filter

The place named on the page is the place the data is filtered to, and the filter carries across pages.

Every source cited

Datasets, standards and research are credited with licences in CITATIONS.md.

These are written rules for every contributor and coding agent (CLAUDE.md) · 8

Civic data only works if people can trust it, so these are written rules in the repository, for every contributor and every AI coding agent. First, no made-up numbers: when data is missing the app shows that it's missing, because a made-up dollar amount is worse than nothing. Second, evidence on every match: if a result shows up because of a search term, the tile shows the real passage. Third, the label on the page always matches the filter actually applied. Fourth, every dataset and method is cited with its licence.

Open by design

Anyone can check it, use it or build on it

  • Open code. Apache 2.0 on GitHub, with issues, pull requests and reviews in the open.
  • Open data. Datasets published on Hugging Face for download and reuse.
  • Open to AI assistants. An MCP server lets assistants such as Claude query the data directly.
  • Open standards. Meeting data compatible with the Council Data Project; place IDs from the Open Civic Data standard (OCD-ID).

Public records should stay public when they are made easier to use.

github.com/getcommunityone/open-navigator · 9

Everything is open. The code is Apache 2.0, and the work happens in public issues and pull requests. The datasets are published on Hugging Face. There is an MCP server, so an AI assistant can query the data directly instead of guessing. And the data follows existing civic standards, like the Council Data Project's meeting model and Open Civic Data identifiers, so it can connect to other civic tools rather than becoming another silo.

This quarter, most of the work goes to data centers, because that's where residents are asking the hardest questions: what's being built near me, who approved it, what it will do to my power bill, and whether the promised jobs are real. We start with two of those.
